Module 01 · Adjectives

Do they work as equals?

Use the and test to find coordinate adjectives.

The “and” test

Put and between the describing words. If it sounds natural and keeps the meaning, separate those adjectives with a comma.

a quiet, peaceful beach → a quiet and peaceful beach
a small old wooden boat → “small and old and wooden” feels like a list of layers, so no commas.
Check each gap independently. In “a beautiful, quiet old village,” only the first pair passes the test.
Five no-comma reminders
1 · Quantity firstNo comma after a how-many word
many tall trees
2 · Colour, age, materialNo comma before these noun details
a small blue car
3 · Colour shadesKeep shade names together
a light blue sky
4 · Last adjective + nounNever split the last descriptor from its noun
a quiet, peaceful beach
5 · Noun-forming wordDon’t split a word that belongs with the noun
a new football pitch
Quantity words includemany, several, some, a few, both, all, numbers

Module 02 · Phrase placement

Where is the phrase?

Position determines the comma pattern.

A

Opening · comma after

Always place a comma after an opening phrase—short or long.

In the background, there are trees.
After the long journey, we rested.
B

Middle · commas on both sides

A phrase that adds information about the subject is bracketed by two commas. One comma alone is a mistake.

The market, located in the old city, attracts visitors.
C

End · usually no comma

If the ending tells where, when, or how, leave it attached without a comma.

The family sat together under a large tree.
D

End -ing / -ed action · check its subject

If the action belongs to the opening subject, use a comma before the action.

She finished early, giving her time to relax.

Quick decision

Choose the correctly punctuated opening phrase.

Module 03 · Relative clauses

Three steps. In order.

Stop at the first rule that applies.

1

Does the clause start with THAT?

In this class framework, a clause introduced by that is defining.

→ No commas. Stop here.
2

Is the noun already identified?

A proper name or a possessive such as my or our followed by one unique thing already identifies it.

→ Use commas. Stop here; do not continue to Step 3.
3

For WHO or WHICH: use the WHY test

Is the information essential—a logical reason the noun is identified? No commas. Is it extra context—an illogical reason for identifying it? Use commas.
